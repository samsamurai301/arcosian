# Premium and indie case studies

Fifteen deconstructions of games that won on design, craft and identity rather than on user-acquisition budgets. Most were made by solo developers or teams of a handful of people, and most sell once (premium), through a subscription catalogue, or not at all. They cover atmosphere-led arcade (Alto's Odyssey, Downwell), authored puzzle and narrative (Monument Valley, Florence, Reigns), the roguelite family (Balatro, Slay the Spire, Dead Cells, Shattered Pixel Dungeon), systemic sims (Stardew Valley, Mini Metro, Pocket City), a gentle social service (Sky: Children of the Light), a viral daily puzzle (Wordle) and a subscription-funded puzzle RPG (Grindstone). Read them for three things free-to-play cases rarely teach: **how to scope** a game a tiny team can finish, **how to make a rule set deep** rather than wide, and **how to earn word of mouth** with identity instead of paid installs. Each case uses the 16-lens format from `SKILL.md`. Where a game has no live economy, the Economy lens describes the run economy (resources inside a run) or says plainly that there is none, and the ontology encoding uses `progression.tracks` instead of currencies. Currency `kind` follows the `SKILL.md` conventions. Sales figures are left out unless sourced; market facts that move are in section 17.

**Contents.** Sections 1–15 are case studies (index below). Section 16 is a cross-case synthesis with decision tables and checklists. Section 17 lists volatile facts with sources.

## 1. Alto's Odyssey

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Team Alto, a collaboration between Snowman (Toronto) and artist-designer Harry Nesbitt. Sequel to *Alto's Adventure* (2015).
- Release: iOS February 2018, Android later in 2018. An expanded edition, *Alto's Odyssey: The Lost City*, launched on Apple Arcade in 2021 (verify).
- Genre: endless sandboarding runner (side-scrolling). Business model: premium on iOS at launch; the Android release used a different model (verify by platform and edition).
- Scale: both Alto games were widely praised and awarded; sales figures are omitted.

**Core loop**
1. The rider descends automatically; slope and gravity set speed.
2. Tap to jump; hold in the air to backflip. Chaining tricks (flips, grinds, wall rides, balloon bounces) builds a combo, and landing a combo grants a speed boost.
3. Read terrain and avoid obstacles and chasms. A bad landing or a collision ends the run.
4. Collect coins; complete goals (three active at a time).
5. Spend coins on power-ups and items in the workshop (verify the current list); goal levels unlock characters and abilities, including the wingsuit.

**Meta and progression**
- Goal levels in sets of three, which unlock a roster of characters with different handling, new abilities and areas.
- Biomes (dunes, canyons, temples) with dynamic weather and a day-night cycle; a photo mode; **Zen Mode**, which removes score, coins and game over.

**Economy**

| Currency | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coins | soft | runs | workshop items and power-ups |
| Goal level | xp | completed goal sets | characters, abilities, areas |

**Monetization**: a single premium purchase on iOS at launch, with no currency for sale. The Apple Arcade edition is included in the subscription.

**Live-ops**: none in the free-to-play sense. Content arrived through updates and the later expanded edition.

**Retention hooks**: goal triplets; unlocking the next character or ability; beating a combo record; and the calm itself, since players return to unwind.

**Social layer**: minimal: leaderboards and photo sharing.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- One touch with two meanings (tap to jump, hold to flip) gives depth without extra buttons.
- The first goals teach the trick vocabulary; text is minimal; restart is instant; failure is gentle.

**Art and audio identity**: flat-shaded, layered parallax landscapes with gradient skies, dynamic lighting and weather, and an ambient soundtrack with reactive audio. The art direction is the product's identity and its marketing.

**Psychology principles used**: flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990); goal-gradient on goal sets; sensation and submission (the MDA "pastime" aesthetic) as primary aesthetics; low-stakes failure, which lowers anxiety; peak moments from long combos.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- With *Alto's Adventure*, recast the endless runner as an atmospheric experience rather than a high-score treadmill.
- A no-fail Zen Mode in a score-attack genre.
- Dynamic weather and time of day as perceptual variety in a repetitive loop.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- Long-term goals run out; the loop repeats.
- Seen by some as an iterative sequel.
- Procedural terrain occasionally produces crashes that feel unfair.
- The premium price limits reach compared with free runners.

**Lessons for designers**
1. When the mechanic is simple, invest in sensation: art, audio and weather can be the core value.
2. Tap versus hold gives two verbs from one input.
3. A no-fail mode serves a different motivation (relaxation) at almost no cost.
4. Goal triplets give an endless game structure and a sense of progress.
5. Procedural terrain needs readability rules (telegraph chasms, limit blind drops) so failure feels fair.

**Analogy hooks**
- Zen Mode → puzzle games (no timer, no fail), idle games (pure ambience mode).
- Tap/hold dual meaning → platformers, golf swings, fishing casts.
- Goal triplets → idle, puzzle and hybrid-casual metas.
- Weather and time of day → any repetitive core loop that needs perceptual freshness.

## 2. Monument Valley

**Snapshot**
- Developer: ustwo games (London); lead designer and artist Ken Wong.
- Release: April 2014 on iOS, Android shortly after. A paid expansion, *Forgotten Shores*, followed in November 2014. *Monument Valley 2* (2017) and *Monument Valley 3* (December 2024, launched through Netflix Games) continued the series.
- Genre: narrative puzzle built on impossible geometry. Business model: premium, plus a paid expansion.
- Scale: ustwo published a "Monument Valley in numbers" breakdown in 2015 (sales, costs, platform split); consult it for figures rather than secondary retellings.

**Core loop**
1. Observe a one-screen isometric structure and the silent princess Ida's goal.
2. Manipulate the architecture: rotate handles, slide platforms, press switches, rotate whole sections.
3. Exploit impossible geometry: paths that connect only from the current viewpoint (in the spirit of Escher and the Penrose triangle) become walkable.
4. Tap to walk Ida along the new path. Crow People patrol and block paths, and can be redirected. Totem, a companion pillar, acts as a moving platform in later chapters.
5. Reach the goal; the level folds closed with a visual flourish; the next chapter opens.

**Meta and progression**
- Ten chapters in the original release and a short arc of about two hours, told through environment and a few lines of text. No score, no stars, no currencies.

**Economy**: none. Progress is the chapter sequence. The scarce resource is the player's attention, and the design spends it on one perceptual insight per level.

**Monetization**: the up-front price; *Forgotten Shores* as a paid in-app expansion; later sequels on new channels (a subscription catalogue for *Monument Valley 3*).

**Live-ops**: none. The game is designed for completion and word of mouth, not retention.

**Retention hooks**: deliberately few. The "hook" is curiosity about the next screen inside a single sitting or a few short sessions.

**Social layer**: none in-game. Every screen works as a shareable image, and awards and press coverage carried discovery.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- No text tutorial. Affordances (handles that look grabbable, glowing paths) and immediate visual feedback teach everything.
- No failure state: the puzzle is perception, not execution, so there is nothing to punish.

**Art and audio identity**: Escher-inspired architecture, pastel palettes and spare geometry, each level composed like a print. Soft ambient music, with interaction sounds that chime as structures rotate into place.

**Psychology principles used**: insight ("aha") problem solving in the Gestalt tradition; curiosity (Loewenstein, 1994); aesthetic pleasure; competence without punishment; perceptual surprise.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Brought perspective-based impossible paths (with a console precedent in *echochrome*, 2008) to touch, where rotating a structure with a finger feels physical.
- Positioned a premium mobile game as an art object, and was unusually open about its numbers afterwards.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- Very short, with little replay value.
- The paid expansion drew a wave of one-star reviews from players who expected free content, despite its small price. This is the best-known precedent for the expectation gap around mobile expansions.
- Piracy and price sensitivity on Android (see ustwo's breakdown).

**Lessons for designers**
1. A short, authored, beautiful arc can be the whole product. Price it as an experience and say how long it is.
2. Teach with affordances and feedback, not text.
3. A fail-free puzzle satisfies when each level holds one perceptual "aha".
4. Announce expansion pricing early, frame it as new content, and expect players to compare its price with the base game rather than with its production cost.
5. Make every screen a screenshot: in premium, the art is the marketing.

**Analogy hooks**
- Perspective-dependent paths → platformers, AR games, camera-rotation puzzles.
- Architecture manipulation → level-based puzzles in any theme (sort, physics, pipes).
- Fail-free "aha" levels → narrative games, kids' games, relaxing puzzle modes.

## 3. Florence

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Mountains (Melbourne), founded by Ken Wong after *Monument Valley*; published by Annapurna Interactive.
- Release: February 2018 on iOS, followed by Android and later PC and Nintendo Switch.
- Genre: interactive story (an "interactive graphic novel"). Business model: premium.
- Recognition: widely reported awards include an Apple Design Award (2018) and a BAFTA Games Award for mobile (2019); verify before quoting. Length about 30–45 minutes.

**Core loop (per vignette)**
1. A short scene from Florence Yeoh's life: a 25-year-old in Melbourne, stuck in routine (work, phone, family calls).
2. A small interaction embodies the scene's feeling: brushing teeth with a scrubbing motion, the commute, assembling the pieces of a speech bubble to speak, unpacking boxes to make room in a shared home, rapid broken fragments during an argument.
3. The form of the interaction changes with the relationship. Early conversation puzzles need many pieces; as the couple grows comfortable, the puzzles get simpler; in conflict they fragment.
4. Advance to the next chapter. The arc covers meeting the cellist Krish, moving in, drifting apart, and Florence rediscovering her art.

**Meta and progression**: about 20 short chapters in one arc. Nothing else.

**Economy**: none.

**Monetization**: the up-front price.

**Live-ops**: none.

**Retention hooks**: designed for one sitting; the pull is narrative, not systemic.

**Social layer**: none in-game; recommendation and press carried it.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- Portrait, one hand, almost no words. Each mechanic is self-explanatory within seconds.
- The speech-bubble puzzle became the signature: you literally assemble what you want to say.

**Art and audio identity**: hand-drawn graphic-novel art in warm colours, and a score (by Kevin Penkin; verify) that carries the emotion in place of voiced dialogue.

**Psychology principles used**: embodied cognition (doing the action shapes the feeling); narrative transportation; empathy; the peak-end rule (Kahneman et al., 1993) with an ending about personal growth rather than romance.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Mechanics as metaphor in a mainstream mobile release: every verb means what the scene means.
- A "mechanic arc": the difficulty of an interaction changes to show a relationship changing.
- Proof that a short premium narrative can succeed on phones.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- Too short for its price in some players' eyes; low interactivity; little replay value; a niche audience.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Choose verbs that mean what the scene means: assembling a speech bubble is finding words.
2. Let a mechanic's difficulty follow the story: parallel arcs make change felt, not told.
3. Short can be complete if the scope is one emotional arc.
4. Music can replace dialogue. Budget for it as a core feature (`audio-direction`).

**Analogy hooks**
- Mechanic arcs → tutorials and story beats in puzzle games (a mechanic that eases as the protagonist gains confidence).
- Conversation-as-puzzle → dialogue systems in RPGs and narrative metas.
- Embodied chores → cosy sims (cooking, cleaning, packing as storytelling).

## 4. Balatro

**Snapshot**
- Developer: LocalThunk (a solo developer in Canada); published by Playstack.
- Release: 20 February 2024 on Windows and consoles, macOS on 1 March 2024, iOS and Android on 26 September 2024 (premium), with an Apple Arcade edition (*Balatro+*); a Nintendo Switch 2 version followed on 25 February 2026 ([Wikipedia: Balatro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balatro)).
- Inspirations (developer's account): the project began as an online version of the Cantonese card game Big Two; it became a single-player roguelike deckbuilder after LocalThunk watched Northernlion stream *Luck Be a Landlord*, and he played *Slay the Spire* near the end of development to understand its controls. He has described the 52-card deck as a shared design tool refined over centuries (same source).
- Genre: roguelike deckbuilder built on poker hands. Business model: premium, with no ads or IAP on mobile (verify).
- Recognition: won Best Independent Game, Best Debut Indie Game and Best Mobile Game at The Game Awards 2024. Sales are widely reported in the millions (verify current figures).

**Core loop**
1. Each ante has three blinds (Small, Big and Boss). A blind sets a score target and gives a fixed number of hands and discards.
2. Draw a hand of eight cards; play up to five as a poker hand, or discard to dig for better cards.
3. **Score = chips × mult.** The hand type sets base chips and mult; scored cards add chips; Jokers add chips, add mult, or multiply mult.
4. Beat the target before the hands run out. Earn money: a blind reward, a bonus for each unused hand, and interest on savings (by default $1 per $5 held, capped at $5).
5. Visit the shop: Jokers (five slots by default), Planet cards (level up a hand type), Tarot cards (modify cards' suit, rank or enhancement), Spectral cards, vouchers (permanent run upgrades) and booster packs. Rerolling the shop costs money.
6. Boss blinds impose a rule (for example, debuffing a suit). Beat the ante 8 boss to win; endless mode continues.

**Meta and progression**
- Unlocks of Jokers, decks and vouchers discovered across runs; a collection screen; challenges.
- A **stake** ladder per deck: eight difficulty levels, each adding a cumulative modifier.

**Economy (inside a run)**

| Currency | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Money ($) | soft | blind rewards, unused hands, interest, selling Jokers and cards | shop items, rerolls |
| Hands per round | energy | fixed per blind (modifiable) | each played hand |
| Discards per round | energy | fixed per blind (modifiable) | each discard |
| Hand levels | xp | Planet cards | none (raise base chips and mult) |

**Monetization**: the up-front price; the subscription for the Apple Arcade edition. No randomised purchases.

**Live-ops**: free content updates, including collaboration packs that re-skin face cards with characters from other indie games (verify the current list).

**Retention hooks**: "one more run" (a new run starts in a tap); discovery of Joker synergies; the stake ladder; collection completion.

**Social layer**: none in-game; a strong streaming and community culture built on sharing absurd builds and scores.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- A single screen; every Joker has a tooltip; the scoring animation counts chips, then mult, step by step, which makes multiplicative scoring legible. Exceeding the target triggers an escalating fire effect.
- Turn-based and pause-safe: well suited to interrupted mobile sessions. The touch version supports tap selection and drag reordering.

**Art and audio identity**: CRT-style pixel art over a swirling psychedelic background, chunky card art, and a hypnotic looping soundtrack. Numeric feedback is the main "juice".

**Psychology principles used**: competence through combinatorial discovery; variable reward from shop offers; the build fantasy of exponential growth (multiplicative modifiers); low learning cost from familiar poker hands; risk and reward in saving for interest versus spending now; loop closure, since the end of one run invites the next.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Fused poker hand evaluation with roguelike deckbuilding, where Jokers are rule modifiers that stack additively and multiplicatively.
- Interest on savings as a constant economic decision between rounds.
- A huge build space from a tiny interface, made by one developer.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- Its playing-card and casino imagery drew age-rating trouble. Per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balatro), PEGI discussed the game with the publisher before release and an initial 18+ was lowered to 3+; shortly after release PEGI re-rated it 18+ for prominent gambling imagery (the game was pulled from sale in some territories, with a removal dated to 1 March 2024); the publisher appealed, and in February 2025 PEGI reclassified it as PEGI 12. The game has no real-money gambling, but the theme invites scrutiny. Other boards' ratings (ESRB, USK, ACB) were not checked.
- Some runs hinge on shop luck; high stakes can feel opaque; there is little narrative.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Start from a rule set players already know (poker hands), then let modifiers break it.
2. Separate additive from multiplicative effects, and show the arithmetic step by step so players can learn it.
3. Put an economic decision between rounds: saving should be a strategy.
4. Keep mobile versions single-screen and turn-based: instant resume, no timing pressure.
5. Check how a theme will be rated before committing to casino imagery (`legal-ethics`).

**Analogy hooks**
- Chips × mult scoring → match-3 combos, tower-defense damage, idle multipliers.
- Interest on savings → any run-based shop (roguelites, auto-battlers).
- Rule-breaking Jokers → solitaire variants, puzzle modifiers, level mutators.
- Boss blinds with rules → level modifiers in any level-based game.

## 5. Stardew Valley

**Snapshot**
- Developer: ConcernedApe (Eric Barone, working alone for most of development). Originally published on PC by Chucklefish, later self-published.
- Release: PC February 2016; iOS October 2018, Android March 2019; consoles in between. Major free updates followed, including 1.5 (2020) and 1.6 (2024).
- Genre: farming sim with RPG systems, in the *Harvest Moon* tradition. Business model: premium; updates have been free. Barone has publicly said he does not intend to charge for DLC or updates to the game (verify the exact statement).
- Scale: reported sales in the tens of millions across platforms (verify the latest figure).

**Core loop (one in-game day, about 14 real minutes)**
1. Wake at 6 a.m. with full energy; check the weather, luck and tips on TV.
2. Water and harvest crops and tend animals; every tool use costs energy.
3. Spend the rest of the day on one or two of: mining, fishing, foraging, socialising (gifts and conversations), crafting, shopping.
4. Put goods in the shipping bin; they sell overnight for gold.
5. Sleep before 2 a.m. (passing out costs gold), then plan the next day with the new gold.

Seasons last 28 days; crops are seasonal, and festivals fall on fixed calendar days.

**Meta and progression**
- The **Community Center bundles**: collection goals (crops, fish, forage, artisan goods) that restore the town and unlock areas. An alternative corporate route buys the same unlocks with gold.
- Five skills (farming, mining, foraging, fishing, combat) with profession choices at levels 5 and 10; relationships with hearts, events and marriage; the Mines (120 floors) and Skull Cavern; a museum collection; Ginger Island (from 1.5); co-op multiplayer.

**Economy**

| Currency | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold | soft | shipping crops, artisan goods, fish, minerals; quests | seeds, buildings, animals, tool and house upgrades |
| Energy | energy | sleep (full refill), food | tool use, some actions (daily cap; food raises the effective limit) |
| Resources (wood, stone, ores, crops) | material | farming, mining, foraging | crafting, buildings, bundles |
| Artisan goods | material | converters (kegs, preserves jars, cheese presses) | shipping (worth more than raw goods), gifts, bundles |
| Friendship and skill XP | xp | gifts, conversations, actions | heart events, professions |

**Monetization**: the up-front price only.

**Live-ops**: none in the free-to-play sense. Large free updates every few years renewed press, sales and player return.

**Retention hooks**: "one more day" (the day clock closes a loop and opens the next); crops ready tomorrow; seasonal planning; relationships and heart events; bundle goals; the festival calendar.

**Social layer**: co-op multiplayer on a shared farm; a large modding community on PC; no competitive layer.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- The day clock and energy bar make every day a planning puzzle with natural session ends.
- The mobile version adapted controls for touch (tap-to-act, optional joystick); combat and fishing are the least comfortable verbs on touch. The PC original saves only overnight; check the mobile save behaviour before treating it as interruption-safe.

**Art and audio identity**: 16-bit-style pixel art with seasonal palettes and a soundtrack composed by Barone; the cosy tone is consistent across art, writing and music.

**Psychology principles used**: autonomy (choose how to spend each day); competence (farm efficiency); relatedness (villagers with stories); goal-gradient (bundles); Zeigarnik (crops ripening tomorrow); a fresh start every morning.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Revived the *Harvest Moon* formula with deeper RPG systems and modern quality of life, at a scope one person delivered.
- Bundles as a collection-driven goal map over an open sim.
- Free major updates as a premium long-tail strategy.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- A slow, grind-heavy path to "perfection"; early-game energy and time pressure; imprecise touch combat and fishing.
- A solo-developer update cadence, by design, means long gaps.

**Lessons for designers**
1. A visible day clock plus energy gives an open sim rhythm: each day is a planning puzzle with a natural stopping point.
2. Collection bundles turn exploration into a map of goals.
3. Many light systems (farm, mine, fish, social) serve many motivations; each must be simple on its own.
4. Converters (raw goods → artisan goods over time) add economic depth cheaply.
5. Free updates extend a premium game's tail and build goodwill.

**Analogy hooks**
- Day clock with energy → any sim or RPG meta that needs session shape.
- Bundles → renovation metas in puzzle games, collection books in RPGs.
- Seasonal crops → rotating event content.
- Converters → idle and production games (Hay Day's buildings in `references/casual-social-sim.md`).

## 6. Slay the Spire

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Mega Crit (Seattle; Casey Yano and Anthony Giovannetti).
- Release: Early Access November 2017, full release January 2019 on PC; iOS June 2020, Android February 2021. A sequel was announced in 2024; Mega Crit moved its development to Godot after leaving Unity during the 2023 runtime-fee controversy (verify the sequel's release status).
- Genre: roguelike deckbuilder. Business model: premium.
- Scale: widely credited with popularising the roguelike-deckbuilder genre; sales figures are omitted.

**Core loop**
1. Pick a path on a branching map whose nodes are monsters, elites, unknown events, rest sites, merchants and treasure.
2. Combat: draw five cards; spend three energy per turn on attacks, skills and powers. Every enemy shows its **intent** for its next turn (attack and amount, block, buff, debuff).
3. After a win: choose one of three card rewards, **or skip**; collect gold, sometimes potions; elites drop relics (permanent run modifiers).
4. Rest sites: heal or upgrade a card. Merchants sell cards, relics and potions, and remove cards for a fee.
5. Defeat each act's boss (three acts, plus a hidden final act unlocked with keys). Death ends the run.

**Meta and progression**
- Four characters (Ironclad, Silent, Defect, and the Watcher, added in a free update) with unlockable cards and relics.
- **Ascension** levels 1–20: each win unlocks the next level, which adds one cumulative difficulty modifier.
- A seeded daily climb with modifiers and a leaderboard; custom runs.

**Economy (inside a run)**

| Currency | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold | soft | combat rewards, events | merchant purchases, card removal |
| HP | energy | max HP; heals at rest sites, relics, potions | damage taken (carries between fights) |
| Energy per turn | energy | 3 per turn by default (relics add more) | card costs |
| Potions | material | combat rewards, merchants (limited slots) | one-off effects |
| Cards and relics | material | rewards, merchants, events | deck and run build |

**Monetization**: the up-front price only.

**Live-ops**: the daily climb; content and balance updates through Early Access and after launch.

**Retention hooks**: run variety; the Ascension ladder; unlocks; the daily climb; the "one more run" loop.

**Social layer**: a daily-climb leaderboard; strong streaming and community analysis culture.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- **Intent icons** make enemy plans readable, turning a random fight into a solvable turn.
- Card text is short and keyword-based; the mobile port uses tap-to-select and drag-to-target at phone-readable sizes. Turn-based play is fully interruption-safe.

**Art and audio identity**: functional, hand-drawn art that prioritises readable iconography over spectacle (often called plain, rarely called unclear).

**Psychology principles used**: competence through planning with good information; autonomy in shaping the deck and choosing risk on the map; variable reward in card choices; loss aversion through HP carried between fights.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Combined deckbuilding (descended from tabletop deckbuilders such as *Dominion*, 2008) with roguelike runs and a node map, the template for a genre.
- **Enemy intent** as a transparent threat.
- **Pick one of three or skip**, which makes deck size a resource.
- Balancing from Early Access telemetry (card pick rates, win rates), described by Anthony Giovannetti in a GDC 2019 talk on metrics-driven design and balance.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- Plain art; a steep learning curve; some runs feel decided by early draws or relics.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Show the enemy's next move. Transparent threats turn randomness into planning.
2. Include "skip" in reward choices: the deck's size and focus are resources.
3. Let the map set risk: elites for power, safe paths for survival.
4. Balance with telemetry. Track pick rate and win rate per card and relic, then act on outliers.
5. A cumulative difficulty ladder extends a finished premium game for hundreds of hours.

**Analogy hooks**
- Intent telegraphs → boss telegraphs in action games, enemy counters in puzzle RPGs (Grindstone, section 15).
- Pick-or-skip drafts → hybrid-casual drafts (Archero in `references/hyper-hybrid-casual.md`), auto-battlers.
- Branching node map → puzzle meta maps, roguelite campaigns.
- Ascension → any premium game's endgame.

## 7. Dead Cells

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Motion Twin (Bordeaux, a worker cooperative); later development by Evil Empire.
- Release: Early Access May 2017, full release August 2018 on PC and consoles; iOS August 2019 and Android June 2020 (mobile port by Playdigious). Major updates concluded in 2025 (verify).
- Genre: roguelite action-platformer with metroidvania progression ("roguevania"). Business model: premium with paid DLC expansions.
- Scale: widely reported multi-million sales; figures omitted.

**Core loop**
1. Start a run in the Prisoners' Quarters with basic gear.
2. Fight through levels assembled procedurally from hand-made rooms, choosing among branching biomes.
3. Collect gold, scrolls of power (each raises one of three stat colours: Brutality, Tactics, Survival), weapons and skills, blueprints and **cells**.
4. Between biomes, spend cells at the Collector on permanent unlocks (blueprinted items, flask charges, gold retention and more). **Unspent cells are lost on death.**
5. Die: lose the run's items, keep unlocks and runes, and start again.

**Meta and progression**
- **Runes**: permanent abilities taken from elites and bosses (for example, vines to climb or teleport points) that open new routes, the metroidvania keys of the run structure.
- **Boss Cells**: difficulty tiers (0–5) that add enemies, reduce healing and open new areas.
- Blueprint unlocks, mutations, outfits, and DLC biomes and bosses.

**Economy**

| Currency | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cells | material | enemies (in-run) | Collector unlocks between biomes (lost on death if unspent) |
| Gold | soft | enemies, chests (in-run) | shops, doors; partly kept via unlocks |
| Blueprints | material | enemy drops | unlock new items in the pool (cells required) |
| Scrolls of power | xp | level loot | stat colours for the run |
| Boss Cells | xp | defeating the final boss | difficulty tiers |

**Monetization**: the up-front price plus paid DLC expansions, including a crossover expansion; the mobile versions follow the same model.

**Live-ops**: years of free updates (biomes, weapons, systems) alongside paid DLC, with public roadmaps and community feedback since Early Access.

**Retention hooks**: the unlock tree; new paths opened by runes; the Boss Cell ladder; build variety.

**Social layer**: none in-game; speedrunning and streaming communities.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- Tight, responsive controls (dodge roll, combo timing) are the core of the feel.
- The mobile port added virtual buttons, an automatic-attack option and controller support, an example of assist options as accessibility for touch.

**Art and audio identity**: pixel art produced through a 3D pipeline: characters are modelled and animated in 3D, then rendered to pixel art, which gives fluid animation for a small team (described by Motion Twin's art director in a widely read 2018 article). Impactful hit feedback and a strong soundtrack.

**Psychology principles used**: competence through skill mastery; variable reward from drops; loss aversion from spend-or-lose cells; goal-gradient toward unlocks; autonomy in route choice.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Hybridised roguelite runs with metroidvania permanent abilities that open routes.
- A 3D-to-pixel-art animation pipeline.
- A long Early Access shaped by community feedback; a premium action game ported carefully to touch.

**Weaknesses and criticism**
- High difficulty; touch controls are less precise than a controller; unlock grind; runs long for short mobile sessions.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Make some permanent unlocks change where you can go (runes), not only how strong you are.
2. Spend-or-lose currencies at checkpoints create tension and pacing.
3. Procedural assembly of hand-made rooms balances variety and quality (`procedural-generation`).
4. On mobile, add assists (automatic attacks, aim help) to keep action accessible.
5. A pipeline innovation (3D to pixel) can buy animation quality for a small team.

**Analogy hooks**
- Runes as keys → roguelite and puzzle metas (abilities that open old levels in new ways).
- Spend-or-lose checkpoints → runner banking, idle "cash in" decisions.
- Room-assembly generation → level generation in platformers and dungeon crawlers.
- Difficulty cells → any difficulty ladder.

## 8. Mini Metro

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Dinosaur Polo Club (Wellington, New Zealand; brothers Robert and Peter Curry).
- Release: Early Access 2014, full PC release November 2015, mobile 2016. A sibling game, *Mini Motorways*, launched on Apple Arcade in 2019 and on PC later.
- Genre: minimalist transit strategy (a puzzle-sim hybrid). Business model: premium.
- Scale: sales figures omitted.

**Core loop**
1. Stations appear on a real city's map as shapes (circle, triangle, square, then rarer shapes).
2. Draw lines between stations with a finger; trains run automatically; passengers, drawn as small shapes, travel to any station of their shape.
3. Each week, choose one of two upgrades: a new line, a carriage, a locomotive, a tunnel or bridge (river crossings), or an interchange.
4. Stations keep spawning faster; reroute lines to absorb demand.
5. The game ends when one station stays overcrowded for too long. Score is passengers delivered.

**Meta and progression**: cities unlocked by score; modes (normal, endless, extreme with fixed lines, creative); daily challenges.

**Economy (inside a run)**

| Resource | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lines, locomotives, carriages, tunnels/bridges, interchanges | material | starting kit, weekly upgrade choice | placed on the map (can be moved, except in extreme mode) |
| Passengers delivered | xp | trains reaching matching stations | score, city unlocks |

**Monetization**: the up-front price; cities and modes added through updates.

**Live-ops**: daily challenges and content updates.

**Retention hooks**: score chasing, city unlocks, the daily challenge, and the elegance of a well-designed network.

**Social layer**: leaderboards.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- Drawing a line is the whole interface. Pause at any time to replan, which is essential on mobile where interruptions are certain.
- Colour-blind and night modes; shapes, not colours, carry the routing information.

**Art and audio identity**: visual language taken from transit-map design (the Harry Beck London Underground diagram tradition): clean lines, 45-degree angles, flat colours. A generative soundtrack by Disasterpeace in which lines, trains and stations produce notes, so the music grows with the network.

**Psychology principles used**: competence and flow; mounting pressure (a progressive difficulty curve with no ceiling); the satisfaction of a tidy system; aesthetic pleasure.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Reduced a complex simulation to shapes and lines while keeping its decisions.
- Generative audio driven by system state.
- Pressure as difficulty: constant growth until collapse makes the score a measure of how long you manage entropy.

**Weaknesses and criticism**: inevitable failure frustrates some players; limited long-term progression; depth plateaus for experts.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Abstract a system until only the decisions remain (shapes are destinations).
2. Escalating, unavoidable pressure turns the score into "how long did you cope".
3. Allow pause and replanning on mobile.
4. Make feedback musical by generating audio from system state (`audio-direction`).
5. Weekly two-option upgrades create strategy with almost no UI.

**Analogy hooks**
- Shapes as routing rules → logistics, sorting and delivery puzzles.
- Weekly upgrade choice → tower defense, survival builders.
- System-driven music → any sim or puzzle with growing complexity.
- Endless pressure → survival modes in arcade and puzzle games.

## 9. Reigns

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Nerial (François Alliot); published by Devolver Digital.
- Release: August 2016 on mobile and PC. Spin-offs include *Reigns: Her Majesty* (2017) and *Reigns: Game of Thrones* (2018), with later entries in the series.
- Genre: swipe-based narrative strategy. Business model: premium.
- Scale: sales figures omitted.

**Core loop**
1. A character presents a card: a request, a dilemma or an event.
2. Swipe left or right to answer. While the card is held, dots appear above the four meters (Church, People, Army, Treasury) that the choice will change; a larger dot means a larger change, **but the direction is hidden**.
3. Release; the meters move.
4. If any meter hits zero **or** full, the monarch dies (too much power is as fatal as too little).
5. The reign ends; the next monarch of the dynasty takes the throne, and the deck carries on.

**Meta and progression**
- Years reigned and dynasty length as score; objectives that unlock new cards and story threads; long-running mysteries that span many reigns; a collection of distinct deaths.

**Economy**

| Resource | Kind | Sources | Sinks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Church, People, Army, Treasury | material | card outcomes | card outcomes; death at 0 or at the maximum |
| Years reigned | xp | each card answered | score and unlock conditions |

The four meters are pools with failure at both ends, a homeostasis design rather than an accumulation design.

**Monetization**: the up-front price.

**Live-ops**: none; the series continued through spin-offs.

**Retention hooks**: curiosity about new cards and endings; short reigns that invite another; death collection.

**Social layer**: none.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- One gesture, two options, borrowed from dating apps; the rules are learned in the first reign.
- Partial information (which meters move, not which way) makes each swipe a judgement rather than arithmetic.
- Portrait and one-handed; a reign fits into a few minutes.

**Art and audio identity**: flat, stylised character portraits with a muted palette, dry humour in the writing, and a minimal soundtrack.

**Psychology principles used**: homeostasis (keep four values mid-range); curiosity; dark humour; death as continuation, which removes the frustration of failure; decisions under partial information.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- The swipe as a game verb.
- Bounded meters with death at both ends.
- Narrative delivered through a deck of cards with dynasty continuity across failures.

**Weaknesses and criticism**: randomness in card order; repetition once most cards are seen; agency limited to binary choices.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Two-sided meters (failure at zero and at full) create balance decisions from binary inputs.
2. Show partial information (which values change, not how) to keep choices thoughtful.
3. Make death a continuation (a dynasty) and failure becomes story.
4. Borrow gestures players already know from non-game apps.

**Analogy hooks**
- Two-sided meters → relationship systems, colony morale, city happiness.
- Swipe event cards → events in idle, strategy and management games.
- Dynasty continuity → meta-narrative in roguelites.

## 10. Downwell

**Snapshot**

- Scale: sales figures omitted; widely cited as a model of mobile-first action design.

**Core loop**

4. Collect gems; duck into side rooms (shops, and time-frozen bubbles holding gems or weapon modules).

**Meta and progression**: unlockable **styles** that change the upgrade pool and shop behaviour, and colour **palettes** unlocked by cumulative play.

**Economy (inside a run)**

**Monetization**: the up-front price.

**Live-ops**: none.

**Retention hooks**: run variety from upgrades and weapon modules; combo records; palette and style unlocks.

**Social layer**: none.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- Designed for the phone held upright: a tall, narrow view with three inputs (left, right, jump/shoot). On PC it runs in a vertical window by default.
- One button with two meanings (jump on the ground, shoot in the air) makes the controls learnable in seconds.

**Art and audio identity**: a three-colour palette (black, white and red by default) for instant readability at speed, crunchy chiptune effects and strong screen shake.

**Psychology principles used**: flow; risk and reward (staying airborne for combos versus landing safely); competence through execution.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- A phone-first vertical action game that also earned respect on PC.
- An attack that is also movement (gunboots slow the fall), so every shot spends a resource with two uses.

**Weaknesses and criticism**: high difficulty; short total content; on-screen touch buttons are less precise than physical ones.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Design for how the phone is held: vertical descent suits portrait one-hand play.

3. Tie attacking to movement so every action is a trade-off.
4. Restrict the palette to make threats readable at speed (`art-direction`).

**Analogy hooks**
- Recoil movement (shooting changes motion) → platformers, space shooters, golf-like physics.
- Vertical descent → endless fallers, drop puzzles.
- Airborne combos → runner trick systems (Alto's Odyssey, section 1).

## 11. Sky: Children of the Light

**Snapshot**

## 12. Shattered Pixel Dungeon

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Evan Debenham (Shattered Pixel), building on *Pixel Dungeon* by Oleg Dolya ("Watabou"), an open-source roguelike from 2012.
- Release: Android 2014; later iOS and desktop, including Steam in 2022 (verify dates).

**Monetization**: free on mobile; optional supporter purchases; paid desktop versions; the source code is public.

**Live-ops**: large free updates over many years (new classes, reworked regions, talents) with public changelogs and developer blog posts.

**Retention hooks**: build variety; mastery; challenges; seeds and daily runs; class and badge completion.

**Social layer**: an active community and many open-source forks ("pixel dungeon" variants) built on the code.

**Psychology principles used**: competence; discovery through identification; risk management under permadeath; autonomy in build decisions.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- Made the traditional roguelike work on touch by streamlining commands and systems.
- Sustained a long-running game through open-source development and fair, optional monetization.
- Design transparency through public changelogs and developer writing.

**Weaknesses and criticism**: hard; permadeath frustrates newcomers; niche visuals; the free-mobile versus paid-desktop split confuses some players.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Strip a deep genre's controls to a handful of touch actions without stripping its decisions.
2. Turn-based play is mobile-native: perfect interruption safety.
3. Unidentified items make knowledge a form of progression inside a run.
4. Scarce, guaranteed upgrade resources create the run's big strategic decision.
5. A free, open-source game can be sustained by supporter purchases and paid desktop versions.

**Analogy hooks**
- Item identification → mystery mechanics in puzzle and RPG games.
- Scarce upgrade scrolls → gear investment choices in hybrid-casual metas.
- Turn-based roguelike structure → puzzle-roguelite hybrids (Grindstone, section 15).

## 13. Wordle

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Josh Wardle, a software engineer, originally as a gift for his partner (widely reported).
- Release: public web release in October 2021; viral in late 2021 and early 2022. The New York Times acquired it in January 2022 for a reported "low seven figures".
- Genre: daily word puzzle. Business model: free; under the New York Times it sits inside the NYT Games product as a funnel toward subscriptions (verify the current ad and subscription arrangement).
- Scale: player counts widely reported at the peak; omitted here.

**Core loop**
1. Once a day, every player gets the same five-letter answer.
2. Type a valid five-letter guess (six attempts).
3. Each letter turns green (right letter, right place), yellow (in the word, wrong place) or grey (not in the word).
4. Deduce, refine, solve or fail.
5. Share a spoiler-free grid of coloured squares, then wait until tomorrow.

**Meta and progression**: a streak and statistics (games played, win rate, guess distribution); a hard mode in which revealed hints must be used.

**Economy**: none. The scarce resource is time: one puzzle per day.

**Monetization**: none at launch; under the new owner, a funnel into a games subscription (verify).

**Live-ops**: the daily puzzle is the live operation. After the acquisition, editors curate the answer list.

**Retention hooks**: the streak; the daily ritual; comparing grids with friends and family.

**Social layer**: the share grid. Players first improvised coloured-square grids by hand before Wardle added a share button (widely reported). Because everyone solves the same word, the game becomes a shared conversation in group chats and on social media.

**UX and onboarding notables**
- No install, no account, one screen of rules, an on-screen keyboard that shows letter states, and a high-contrast colour option.
- It works in any browser, so a shared link is the whole acquisition funnel.

**Art and audio identity**: minimal typography and three colours; the coloured grid is the brand.

**Psychology principles used**: scarcity (one per day; Cialdini); social comparison and shared experience; mastery; appointment; streak loss aversion; the Zeigarnik pull of an unsolved puzzle within the day.

**Innovations (at the time)**
- One puzzle per day, the same for everyone.
- A spoiler-free shareable artefact that shows performance without giving away the answer.
- Frictionless web distribution and an explicit refusal to monetize, which became part of its appeal. It spawned a wave of "-dle" variants.

**Weaknesses and criticism**: deliberately little content per day; many clones; small controversies when answer lists were edited after the acquisition; long-term repetition.

**Lessons for designers**
1. Scarce content (one a day) can raise desire and protect a small game from burnout.
2. Give players a shareable artefact that shows how they did without spoiling the content.
3. The same puzzle for everyone creates conversation, and conversation is distribution.
4. Remove friction: no install, no account, one-screen rules.
5. A streak and statistics are enough retention for a daily ritual; no economy is required.

**Analogy hooks**
- A daily seed for everyone → roguelite daily runs (Slay the Spire's daily climb, section 6), golf, puzzle games.
- Spoiler-free share grids → any level result or run summary.
- Hard mode as an opt-in rule → any puzzle game.

**Snapshot**
- Developer: Capybara Games (Toronto).

**Retention hooks**: stage objectives; gear progression; per-stage challenges.

**Innovations (at the time)**

## 16. Cross-case synthesis for premium and indie

### 16.1 Lineages

```
Runner as a place:     Alto's Adventure (2015) → Alto's Odyssey (2018) → The Lost City (Apple Arcade, 2021)
Perspective puzzle:    echochrome (2008) → Monument Valley (2014) → Monument Valley 2 (2017) → Monument Valley 3 (2024)
Mechanics as metaphor: Monument Valley's lead designer → Florence (2018)
Deckbuilder:           Dominion (tabletop, 2008) → Slay the Spire (2017/2019) → Balatro (poker hands + Jokers, 2024)
Roguelike:             Rogue (1980) → NetHack → Pixel Dungeon (2012) → Shattered Pixel Dungeon (2014)
Farming RPG:           Harvest Moon (1996) → Stardew Valley (2016)
City builder:          SimCity (1989) → SimCity BuildIt (F2P timers, 2014)  vs  Pocket City (premium, no timers, 2018)
Minimal systems:       Mini Metro (2015) → Mini Motorways (2019)
Swipe narrative:       dating-app swipe gesture → Reigns (2016) → spin-offs
Chain puzzle:          Dungeon Raid (2011), Two Dots (2014) → Grindstone (2019)
Daily puzzle:          newspaper crosswords → Wordle (2021) → NYT Games
Social adventure:      Journey (2012) → Sky: Children of the Light (2019)
```

### 16.2 Premium or free-to-play? Decision table

| Factor | Favours premium | Favours free-to-play | Cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content shape | a finite authored arc, or a deep systemic rule set | endless content fed by live-ops | MV, Florence (arc); Balatro, StS, Mini Metro (systems) vs `casual-social-sim.md` |
| Depth source | combinatorics and mastery | progression and collection | Balatro, StS vs Archero |
| Team capacity | no live-ops team | a live-ops and economy team | Downwell, Reigns, Wordle vs Sky |
| Platform path | PC/console first, mobile port at near parity | mobile first | Balatro, StS, Dead Cells, Stardew |
| Channel | subscription catalogues, featuring, awards, streamers | paid UA with LTV above CPI | Grindstone, Alto: The Lost City, MV3 |
| Audience | players who distrust IAP in a genre (city builders, farming) | mass casual | Pocket City, Stardew |
| Ethics and trust | no paid randomness, no timers | needs strong ethics rules (`monetization`) | Pocket City positioning |

Rule of thumb: if the game's value is understood in the first 30 minutes and lasts for 10+ hours through systems, premium with free updates is viable. If value accrues through daily habit and social ties, free-to-play is the natural model, but Sky shows it can be done with cosmetics only.

### 16.3 Roguelite meta structures compared

| Game | Persistent power between runs | Persistent unlocks | Difficulty ladder | Seeds / daily |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slay the Spire | none | cards and relics added to pools | Ascension 1–20 | daily climb |
| Balatro | none | Jokers, decks, vouchers discovered | stakes (eight per deck) | enterable seeds |
| Dead Cells | small (flask charges, gold retention) | blueprints; **runes open routes** | Boss Cells 0–5 | daily challenge (verify) |
| Downwell | none | styles and palettes | none | none |
| Shattered Pixel Dungeon | none | badges; classes | challenges | seeds and daily runs |
| Archero (contrast, `hyper-hybrid-casual.md`) | heavy (gear, talents) | heroes, chapters | chapter walls | events |

The premium pattern is to **widen options, not raise power**: unlocks add variety to the pools, and difficulty ladders add challenge. The free-to-play hybrid pattern sells persistent power. A premium roguelite that adds a large power meta loses the "every run is fair" promise; a free-to-play one without power has little to sell except cosmetics.

### 16.4 Scope patterns of small teams

| Team size | Case | Where the content comes from | Scope lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | Stardew Valley | many simple systems plus hand-written villagers, over years | breadth of light systems |
| Solo | Balatro | rules and combinatorics (Jokers × hands × modifiers) | one screen, one rule set |
| Solo | Downwell | procedural wells plus tight rules | three inputs, three colours |
| Solo | Wordle | a word list and one rule | one puzzle a day |
| Tiny | Mini Metro | real city maps plus one routing rule | abstraction |
| Tiny | Reigns | written cards | one gesture |
| Tiny | Pocket City | simulation systems | touch-first simplification |
| Community | Shattered Pixel Dungeon | procedural floors, open-source contributions | streamlined commands |
| Studio | Monument Valley, Florence | authored levels and scenes | deliberately short (about two hours, under one hour) |
| Studio (live) | Sky | authored realms plus seasons | a live team; the costliest model here |

Decision rule: a small team should choose a genre where **systems generate content** (rules, procedural generation, combinatorics), or keep an authored game deliberately short. Avoid the middle: long authored content made by hand is the most expensive per player hour.

### 16.5 PC-to-mobile porting checklist (from Slay the Spire, Dead Cells, Balatro, Stardew Valley)

- [ ] Is play turn-based or pause-safe? If real-time, add assists (automatic attacks, aim help) and controller support (Dead Cells).
- [ ] Replace hover tooltips with long-press; support drag-to-target and tap-to-select (Slay the Spire, Balatro).
- [ ] Minimum touch target 44 pt (Apple HIG) or 48 dp (Material); readable card and tooltip text at phone size without zoom.
- [ ] Save on backgrounding and resume mid-run; never lose progress to a phone call.
- [ ] Session shape: can a meaningful chunk (one fight, one blind, one day) fit in 3–10 minutes?
- [ ] Performance budgets on low- and mid-tier devices (`mobile-performance`).
- [ ] Pricing and parity: decide price relative to PC, and whether DLC and updates ship together.
- [ ] Consider a specialist porting partner (Dead Cells' mobile port by Playdigious).

### 16.6 Earning beyond the first sale

| Model | Case | Effect | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free major updates | Stardew Valley, Slay the Spire (Watcher), Balatro | new sales, press, goodwill | no direct revenue from the update itself |
| Paid DLC expansions | Dead Cells | revenue from engaged players | fragmentation; console and mobile parity |
| Paid mobile expansion | Monument Valley (*Forgotten Shores*) | revenue | backlash from players expecting free content |
| Subscription catalogue | Grindstone, Alto: The Lost City, MV3 | funding up front, polish without IAP | reach limited to subscribers until a wider release |
| Supporter purchases | Shattered Pixel Dungeon | sustainable goodwill | low conversion |
| Cosmetic free-to-play | Sky | long service | FOMO criticism, currency complexity |
| No monetization, then acquisition | Wordle | viral reach | no revenue unless acquired |

### 16.7 Checklist before building a premium or indie game
- [ ] Name the depth source: an authored arc, a systemic rule set or a social world. Pick one primary.
- [ ] Write the one-line identity (art and audio) that will carry marketing without paid UA, and test it with screenshots and a 15-second clip.
- [ ] For roguelites, decide whether persistent power exists. Default for premium: options, not power.
- [ ] Prove the rule set in a paper or grey-box prototype within days (`design-theory`, `concept-lab`).
- [ ] Choose the channel (direct premium, subscription catalogue, PC first) before the business model is locked into the design.
- [ ] Plan the update strategy (free updates, DLC, expansions) and announce it before launch to set expectations.
- [ ] Budget the mobile port or PC version from the start (16.5).
- [ ] Record comparables in `contract.meta.comparables` and set `contract.meta.monetization_model` to `premium` or `subscription`.
